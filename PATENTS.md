## ⚠️ Warning: making this repository public starts a patent clock

**This is not legal advice. Consult a patent attorney before publishing.**

Publishing this repository is a **public disclosure**. The consequences are
immediate, jurisdiction-dependent, and in most of the world irreversible.

### United States — a 12-month deadline

A public disclosure by the inventor starts a **one-year grace period** under
35 U.S.C. § 102(b)(1). A patent application — provisional or non-provisional —
must be filed within **twelve months of the first public disclosure**, or the
disclosure becomes prior art against the inventor's own application and the
invention is unpatentable in the US.

The clock starts at the **first public commit**, not at the first release, tag,
or announcement. Flipping the repository from private to public is the
triggering event.

### Most other countries — no grace period at all

The EPO, China, and most other jurisdictions apply **absolute novelty**. Any
public disclosure before a filing date destroys patentability outright. There
is no grace period to rely on and no way to recover the right afterwards.

**If foreign patent rights matter, file before publishing — not within a year
of publishing.**

### Practical sequence

1. Identify anything potentially patentable.
2. File a provisional application (US) and/or a priority application covering
   the foreign jurisdictions that matter.
3. *Then* make the repository public.

A private repository is not a public disclosure, so keeping it private
preserves every option. Going public closes most of them the moment it happens.

### Prior art

If the decision is to publish without filing, publication is intended to
establish prior art as of the first public commit — which protects the ability
to keep practising the invention, while giving up the ability to patent it
abroad and starting the US twelve-month clock.
